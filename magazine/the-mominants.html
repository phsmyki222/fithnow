THE MOMINANTS™

The stories that shape our world—and become part of who we are.

01 — THE MAKING OF A STORY

Some moments pass. Others remain with us.

A story does not always begin with an extraordinary event. Sometimes it begins with a conversation, a decision, a discovery, a journey or an experience whose significance becomes clear only with time. What first appears to belong to one person can eventually find meaning among families, communities, nations and generations.

People remember the moments that change their understanding of themselves and the world around them. A remarkable achievement, a difficult choice, a defining encounter or an unexpected turn can become part of a much larger account. Its influence may continue long after the original moment has passed.

The stories that endure are not limited to the events that dominate public attention. They can be found in the lives of individuals, the histories of communities, the work of institutions, the achievements of nations and the ideas that alter how people live.

To understand the world is also to understand the stories within it: where they began, what they reveal, whom they affect and why they continue to matter.

02 — BEFORE THE PRESENT

The past is closer than we sometimes imagine.

Long before the modern world took its present form, people were building societies, establishing traditions, exchanging knowledge, preparing food, creating art, competing, trading and finding ways to understand the world around them.

Across ancient civilisations, people developed systems of government, methods of construction, forms of education, practices of trade and expressions of cultural identity. They created objects for everyday use and monuments intended to endure. They recorded events, celebrated achievements and preserved accounts of the people and circumstances that mattered to them.

Their lives were shaped by geography, resources, belief, relationships, conflict, cooperation and the knowledge available to them. These influences produced societies with distinct customs, institutions and ways of organising collective life.

The passage of time did not erase every connection. Languages travelled, foods crossed borders, architectural ideas influenced distant places and knowledge passed between generations. Some customs remained recognisable; others changed as people encountered new circumstances.

The present carries traces of those earlier experiences. The places people inhabit, the words they speak, the institutions they maintain and the customs they preserve all have histories. Understanding those histories gives greater meaning to the world as it exists today.

Every generation receives a story it did not begin.
03 — CULTURE AND THE TABLE

Sometimes a culture introduces itself through food.

A meal can carry an entire history. Its ingredients may reveal the geography of a place. Its preparation may reflect generations of knowledge. Its flavours may tell stories of agriculture, trade, migration, adaptation and encounters between different peoples.

Across the world, food is part of how people welcome guests, celebrate important occasions, maintain family connections and express belonging. Recipes are often preserved through practice and shared experience. A familiar dish can connect someone living far from their birthplace to the people, language and traditions that shaped their early life.

The same ingredient can take on different meanings in different places. Preparation methods, accompanying ingredients and occasions for serving it may vary considerably. The result reflects the environment, history and imagination of the people who have made it part of their culture.

Food also reveals how cultures meet. Trade introduces ingredients to new regions. Migration carries recipes across borders. Communities adapt what they encounter and create dishes that eventually become part of their own local identity.

A table can therefore become a place of cultural memory. It brings people together while preserving knowledge that might otherwise be lost. What is prepared and shared today may carry the influence of people and places from generations ago.

04 — THE QUESTION OF LIFE

Ordinary life contains questions that are anything but ordinary.

People work, travel, communicate, raise families, build relationships, create businesses and make decisions. These activities can become so familiar that their deeper significance is easily overlooked. Yet each is connected to larger questions about how people live and the societies they create.

Why do communities preserve certain customs? Why do societies establish institutions? Why do people travel across continents, devote years to mastering a skill or build something intended to outlast them? What makes one achievement meaningful to an entire nation while another remains a private memory?

There is rarely one answer. Human behaviour is influenced by necessity, knowledge, emotion, environment, opportunity, memory and belief. The same experience can carry different meanings depending on the people involved and the circumstances in which it takes place.

These differences are part of what makes human life worth examining. Beneath familiar routines are decisions, relationships and systems of meaning that influence how people understand themselves and relate to others.

To look closely at everyday life is to recognise the experiences that connect people, the differences that distinguish communities and the choices through which individuals give direction to their lives.

05 — THE TURNING OF THE WORLD

Some of the world's defining changes begin quietly.

A discovery can change how people understand nature. An invention can alter the way an industry works. A new method can improve how a service reaches the public. A decision by an institution can influence the lives of people far beyond its immediate surroundings.

Change takes many forms. It can emerge through scientific research, commercial activity, cultural movements, public institutions, individual achievement or the accumulated work of people addressing a shared problem. Sometimes its effects are immediate. At other times, its significance becomes visible only after years of development and use.

The circumstances surrounding change matter. The resources available, the people involved, the decisions made and the structures through which an idea is put into practice all influence its eventual consequences.

The history of progress is therefore not only a record of celebrated outcomes. It also includes the knowledge, organisation, investment, work and judgement that made those outcomes possible.

Across industries and societies, the effects of important decisions can extend well beyond the moment in which they are made. What begins as a response to a particular circumstance can become part of how the world operates.

06 — ORGANIZATIONS AND THEIR PLACE IN THE WORLD

An institution is measured by the work it carries into the world.

Organizations occupy an important place in modern society. They bring people, expertise, resources and responsibilities together around defined fields of activity. Their character is reflected in the work they undertake, the structures they maintain, the decisions they make and the standards by which their activities are administered.

PHRINUSOMYIS™ is an international sports organization that develops, owns and administers sports competitions. Its work forms part of the wider landscape of organized sport, where competition, administration, sporting identity and the experience of participants and audiences are closely connected.

Within this sporting landscape, ZEAVIQ SPORTS™ stands as an established sporting identity. Its place is understood through its own sporting character, competition formats and the experiences associated with its activities.

Sport itself carries a long human history. Across societies, organised competition has provided settings in which people demonstrate ability, discipline, preparation, judgement and determination. Sporting achievements can represent individuals, communities and nations, while competitions become occasions through which people share moments of collective interest.

Behind every organised competition are decisions about administration, rules, participation, preparation, facilities and presentation. These elements influence the character of the event and the conditions under which participants perform.

The institutions responsible for sport therefore occupy a significant position. Their work affects how competitions are administered, how sporting standards are maintained and how the achievements of participants are recorded and remembered.

07 — SPORT AS HUMAN EXPRESSION

Competition reveals more than the result.

A sporting contest has an immediate outcome. Someone wins, someone loses, a record is established or a performance becomes part of a competition's history. Yet the meaning of sport is rarely exhausted by the final result.

Competition brings preparation, discipline, judgement, courage, coordination and decision-making into view. It places individual and collective effort within a shared setting, where performance can be witnessed, examined and remembered.

For audiences, sport can create a shared experience among people who may otherwise have little in common. A national team can represent more than its players. A local competition can become part of a community's identity. An exceptional performance can remain in public memory long after the event itself.

The design of a competition influences what participants must demonstrate and what audiences are able to appreciate. Its rules, structure, environment and presentation contribute to the character of the sporting experience.

Sport brings together physical ability, preparation, organisation and human ambition. Its significance can be found in the performance itself, in the people who witness it and in the memories that continue after the competition has ended.

A contest may end with a result. Its meaning can continue for generations.
08 — THE STORIES WE CHOOSE TO KEEP

Memory gives experiences a place beyond their own time.

People do not remember every experience equally. Certain moments remain because they changed a relationship, revealed something unexpected, represented an achievement or became connected to a defining period in life.

Communities preserve their stories through photographs, archives, monuments, ceremonies, oral traditions, publications, performances and places of historical importance. Each provides a different way to carry information and meaning into the future.

What is preserved influences what later generations can understand. A carefully recorded account allows people who were not present to examine its circumstances, consider its consequences and learn from the decisions that shaped it.

Preservation also requires judgement. It involves deciding what should be documented, whose experiences should be included, what evidence supports an account and how events can be presented without losing their complexity.

A society's memory is part of its inheritance. It can provide continuity, reveal mistakes, honour achievement and help people understand how the present came to be. The stories that survive influence the questions future generations will ask.

09 — THE FUTURE OF HUMAN EXPERIENCE

The future will be remembered by people who have not yet arrived.

Every generation makes choices within circumstances it did not create. It inherits knowledge, systems, traditions, opportunities and challenges. It then decides what to preserve, what to improve, what to reconsider and what to create.

The world continues to change through new forms of communication, scientific discoveries, commercial developments, cultural expression and advances in technology. Some developments influence a single field. Others affect how entire societies live, work and relate to one another.

Yet invention alone does not determine the quality of what follows. The decisions surrounding its use matter. The way people establish institutions, distribute responsibility, preserve knowledge and maintain public trust influences what future generations inherit.

An idea can become a standard. An achievement can become a reference point. An institution can influence the direction of an industry. A cultural practice can remain meaningful long after the circumstances in which it began have changed.

The future is connected to the choices, work and experiences taking place in the present. Its history is being written through decisions whose full consequences may only become clear with time.

THE STORY DOES NOT END WHERE THE EXPERIENCE ENDS.

A civilisation leaves traces of how it lived. A culture carries the memory of its people. A discovery changes what others understand. An institution gives structure to collective work. A sporting achievement can become part of a nation's shared history.

These stories belong to different places and circumstances. Some are personal. Some are cultural. Some are scientific, commercial, sporting or institutional. Their significance is found in the people they touch, the ideas they carry and the consequences they leave behind.

What connects them is their capacity to remain meaningful beyond the moment in which they occurred. They influence how people understand the past, experience the present and consider what comes next.

The world is shaped by more than events alone. It is shaped by the people who experience them, the decisions that follow and the stories that generations choose to carry forward.

We inherit more than history. We inherit the responsibility of deciding what the next generation will remember.

Every experience leaves something behind. Some become memories, others become foundations, and some alter the direction of what follows. The influence of a person, a culture, an idea or an institution is not measured only by the moment in which it existed, but by what remains because of it and what changes after it. Some leave footprints. Others change the ground.
