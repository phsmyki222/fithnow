THE POWER BEHIND THE WORLD'S GAME

A consequential contest for the leadership of world football is taking shape. With Philippe Diallo entering the race against Gianni Infantino, the approaching FIFA presidential election places governance, institutional influence and the balance of power at the centre of the international game.

BY PHRINUSOMYIS™ NEWSWIRE 17 NOVEMBER 2026 INTERNATIONAL FOOTBALL READING TIME: 7 MIN

The leadership of world football is once again entering a period of significant political consequence. The contest for the presidency of FIFA, the governing institution of international football, has developed into a broader debate about authority, accountability and the relationship between the organisation and the hundreds of football associations that make up its global membership.

The latest development is the entry of Philippe Diallo, president of the French Football Federation, into the presidential race against the incumbent, Gianni Infantino. Diallo's candidacy has received the unanimous backing of the national associations represented on UEFA's Executive Committee and the FIFA Council. The French Football Federation's executive committee subsequently approved his candidacy on 17 November.

The election is scheduled for 18 March 2027 in Rabat, Morocco. It is no longer simply a question of whether the incumbent will seek another term. The central question is whether a challenger can assemble a sufficiently broad coalition across the international football system to make the contest genuinely competitive.

A challenge takes shape

Diallo's entry changes the political landscape of the election. His selection followed a decision by UEFA's leadership to support him as its preferred candidate. The European governing body described its backing as unanimous among the national associations represented at the relevant meeting and presented the decision as a call for renewed unity, trust and stronger governance within world football.

Diallo is not entering the contest as an outsider to football administration. His career has included leadership within the organisation representing French professional clubs, work within the French professional football system and service in FIFA's dispute-resolution structures. He became president of the French Football Federation in 2023 and joined UEFA's Executive Committee in the same year.

His public argument is centred on the way football is governed. At the launch of his campaign in Paris, Diallo called for a more open model of leadership, with greater consultation and a clearer distinction between strategic presidential leadership and the day-to-day administration of the organisation. He has also argued for clubs and players to have a more direct role in decisions affecting the game.

These are campaign positions, not adopted FIFA policy. Their significance lies in the fact that they place governance itself at the centre of the election rather than treating the contest as a routine change of leadership.

The election is becoming a test of institutional confidence: who commands the support of football's members, and what kind of leadership do they want?

The incumbent and the weight of the system

Gianni Infantino has led FIFA since 2016 and is seeking another term. During his presidency, FIFA has expanded the scale of several international competitions and overseen major commercial and financial developments. The organisation's reach, revenue and distribution of development funding give the presidency considerable influence across the global football structure.

Infantino nevertheless enters the approaching election after a period of visible disagreement with parts of the international football community. One major source of criticism was a proposed commercial structure that would have opened a FIFA-related entity to private investment and linked it to future competition revenues. The proposal was abandoned following opposition and concerns about transparency, consultation and the implications for football's governing arrangements.

The controversy contributed to a deterioration in relations between FIFA's leadership and influential European football officials. The French federation withdrew its support for Infantino in September, with Diallo publicly questioning the direction of the organisation and the state of trust between its leadership and stakeholders.

The criticism has not, however, removed the incumbent's institutional advantages. Infantino retains significant support across several regions, and endorsements from major confederations and national associations remain an important factor in the political arithmetic of the election.

Why the numbers matter

FIFA's presidential election is determined by its member associations. The organisation has 211 member associations, each holding a vote at the FIFA Congress. A candidate therefore needs support that extends beyond a single continent or a group of prominent football nations.

UEFA's support gives Diallo a substantial political foundation, but it does not decide the election. The European governing body has 55 member associations, and the wider contest requires candidates to build support across Africa, Asia, North and Central America, the Caribbean, South America and Oceania.

The incumbent's relationships with associations outside Europe are therefore central to the contest. Reporting published ahead of Diallo's campaign launch has described significant support for Infantino in Africa, Asia and South America. Those regional alignments matter because the election is conducted through individual member-association votes, not a simple contest between continental governing bodies.

The distinction is important. Public endorsements, regional alliances and political signals can indicate where support may lie, but they are not the same as a final ballot. The result will be determined by the votes cast at the Congress.

More than a European contest

The political centre of the election will not be confined to Europe. A candidate may receive strong backing from one region and still require a much wider coalition to win the presidency. The influence of national associations across Africa, Asia and the Americas makes international diplomacy an essential part of the campaign.

Diallo has indicated that he has received encouraging signals from outside Europe. He has also spoken about the possibility of working with Japan's Kozo Tashima, another declared candidate, in an effort to consolidate opposition to the incumbent. Any alliance, withdrawal or change in the field of candidates would have to be confirmed by the individuals concerned.

The candidate-registration process is another important part of the contest. Candidates must satisfy FIFA's nomination requirements and undergo eligibility and integrity checks. The deadline for declarations is 18 November 2026, with the final confirmation process continuing into the months before the Congress.

The coming weeks will therefore be decisive in establishing the final field, the shape of the competing campaigns and the extent to which public support translates into confirmed votes.

The question of football's direction

The significance of the election extends beyond the identity of the person who occupies the FIFA presidency. The office carries influence over the direction of international competitions, the distribution of development resources, the commercial structure of the game and the relationship between FIFA and its member associations.

Football is both a sporting institution and a worldwide economic system. Its decisions affect national federations, professional leagues, clubs, players, coaches, sponsors, broadcasters and supporters. Questions about consultation, financial arrangements and the distribution of influence are therefore not confined to administrative offices. They reach the wider structure of the sport.

The competing positions now emerging in the campaign point to different approaches to leadership. Infantino's supporters can point to continuity, established relationships and the scale of FIFA's current development and competition programmes. Diallo's campaign is presenting a case for greater openness, consultation and a different distribution of responsibility within the organisation.

Neither the election result nor the practical consequences of a possible change in leadership can be assumed in advance. Those questions will depend on the candidates' programmes, their ability to secure support and the decisions of the member associations.

What happens next

The immediate focus is the completion of candidate declarations and the confirmation of eligibility. Diallo has announced that he intends to present more detail about his programme, while the other candidates must establish whether they can attract enough support to remain credible contenders.

The campaign will then move into a period of international engagement. Every region will matter. The public positions of confederations and national associations will be watched closely, but the decisive measure will remain the individual votes at the FIFA Congress in Rabat.

For the international football community, the election represents a moment to examine not only the people seeking office but also the institutional structure they intend to lead. The debate concerns the future relationship between FIFA's central leadership and the associations that give the organisation its global character.

The contest is open. Its outcome is not yet known. What is already clear is that the leadership of world football has become one of the most consequential questions in international sport.

The power behind the world's game is ultimately measured by the confidence of the game itself.
